# The Source Check

Takes ONE claim and finds out what it actually rests on. This is the expensive one: it fetches, and
it is the only one of the three that can catch a citation that resolves but no longer says the thing.

**Run it on the claims the Claim Sweep scored worst. Not on all of them.**

---

Here is a claim and the source it is attributed to:

> CLAIM: [paste the claim]
> ATTRIBUTED TO: [paste the citation as the document gives it]

Find what this claim actually rests on. Work in this order and say which step succeeded:

1. **Go to the original.** Not a write-up of it, not a summary, not a press release about it. If the
   document cites a news article about a study, the study is the source and the article is not.
2. **If it is paywalled or dead**, try the Internet Archive, then an institutional or university
   repository. A paywall is not a dead end.
3. **Check the version.** A preprint and a published paper can report different numbers. Searching
   the wrong version for the right figure returns nothing and looks exactly like a fabricated
   citation. Before you declare a figure unsupported, confirm which version was cited.

Then report:

- **The verbatim sentence** carrying the figure, and its locator: page, section, table or timestamp.
  If you cannot quote it, you have not found it.
- **The denominator.** What is the number a percentage OF, and is that the same thing the claim
  says it is.
- **The date**, both of publication and of your retrieval.
- **The method**, and its limits: sample size, population, whether it is an experiment, a survey, an
  observation or somebody's own report about themselves.
- **Whether the claim survives.** One of: `confirmed`, `confirmed with a correction` (say what to
  change), `could not be grounded` (say exactly what you searched, so the next person does not
  repeat it), or `contradicted`.

Two rules that are not optional:

- **If you did not retrieve it, say so.** A claim you are confident about but did not open is
  `recalled`, however sure you are.
- **A failed search is a result.** "Searched these four indexes by phrase, journal and year, found
  nothing" is a finding worth recording. Silence is not.

Argue the strongest case against the claim before you conclude. Where two sources disagree,
give the conditions each held under and say where each is right, rather than picking one.

---

## Why the version trap matters

This is not hypothetical. A figure cited to a published paper was searched for in that paper's
preprint, was not there, and the claim was recorded as unsupported. Both figures were real. They
were in the published version only, and the preprint reports different measures entirely. The audit
nearly pulled a correct slide.
